## A2. Drop performer identification — registered participants only
Face-ID currently attempts to identify everyone in frame, including the performer. This
is redundant: **only registered participants' data is analysed** — in practice, the
parents of the infants wearing EmotiBits (see A8).

- **Action:** remove the performer from the face-ID gallery and from any identity-linked
  output. The gallery becomes registered parents only — one entry per dyad.
- **Benefit:** a smaller gallery raises matching precision and removes false-match
  pressure; it also drops the subject who is furthest from the camera and hardest to
  resolve, so the remaining range budget is spent where it matters.
- **Note:** the performer should still be *detected and tracked* (they are part of the
  scene and needed for crowd-average context) — just not identified, and they should be
  excluded from any audience-level aggregate.

## A3. Extending face-ID range — does a Kalman filter help?

**Short answer: not directly, but it unlocks the fix.**

A Kalman filter estimates and smooths a track's state (position, velocity) and predicts
where a person will be on the next frame. It adds no facial detail. The recognition limit
here is *pixels across the face*, and no state estimator creates pixels — so a Kalman
filter alone will not extend range.

What it *does* fix is the reason the range limit was fatal. See P0-1 below: 373 track IDs
were created for at most 8 people, median track lifetime ~20 s. A Kalman-predicted bbox
with proper association (SORT / ByteTrack / OC-SORT) keeps one person on one track ID
through occlusion and dropped detections. Once tracks are stable you only need **one**
good face frame per track and can propagate that identity across the whole track.

The data says this would work:

| Session | tracks | tracks with ≥1 frame at fill ≥0.85 (ID-capable) | rows covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 26-Jul morning | 373 | **85 (22.8%)** | 48,308 (23.2%) |
| 26-Jul afternoon | 221 | **20 (9.0%)** | 11,088 (7.9%) |

At a ≥0.70 threshold it rises to 28.4% / 11.8% of tracks (34.0% / 13.5% of rows). And
those are *fragments* — merge them back into real person tracks and coverage climbs
sharply, because 373 fragments are really ≤8 people. If each audience member passes the
camera once during the session, sticky identity labels their entire track.

**Ranked interventions, cheapest first:**

0. **Multi-angle enrolment (left / front / right)** — see **A4**. Cheapest of all and
   addresses the largest single block of failures.
1. **Track-level identity voting + embedding aggregation.** Average quality-weighted face
   embeddings across a track instead of matching per frame; assign one identity per track
   with confidence voting. Lowers the effective per-frame threshold. Biggest win for the
   least work — and it depends on the Kalman/association fix above.
2. **Raise capture resolution.** 640×480 is the hard ceiling on this dataset — the median
   head is only ~16 px (morning) and ~11 px (afternoon) tall. See **A5.1**: this is the
   single biggest lever, ahead of every model choice.
3. **ROI upscale before the face model.** Crop the person bbox and upscale to the face
   model's native input rather than running it on the full frame — recovers detail that
   whole-frame downsampling throws away.
4. **Appearance re-ID as a bridge.** A clothing/appearance embedding links fragments of
   the same person across the session even when the face is never visible; combine with
   the one-time close-range face match.
5. **Face restoration / super-resolution** (GFPGAN, CodeFormer, GPEN) on the crop. Real
   gains at small scales but computationally heavy, and these models can *hallucinate*
   identity — validate against known ground truth before trusting any match.
6. **Optical:** longer focal length, or a second narrow-FOV camera covering the seating
   block. Only worth it if 1–4 fall short.

See **A5** for the model/resolution review, **A6** for accuracy techniques, and **A7** for
two ways to get identity without relying on faces at all.

**Also worth doing regardless:** enrol registered audience members at a close-range
station at the door. A clean, high-resolution gallery image raises match confidence at
every distance, and A2 makes that list short and well-defined. Enrol from **three angles**
— see A4, which is probably the single biggest win available.

## A4. U-shaped seating: multi-angle (left / front / right) enrolment

**Yes — and the data says this matters more than distance for the side seats.**

Splitting every detection by horizontal position in the frame and looking *only* at rows
already large enough to identify (bbox fill ≥ 0.85, i.e. inside the ~2.2 m range):

| horizontal zone | close-range rows | ID rate |
|---|---|---|
| far-left | 303 | 0.0% |
| left | 853 | 0.6% |
| **centre** | **3,280** | **33.0%** |
| right | 987 | **0.0%** |
| far-right | 1,747 | **0.0%** |

3,890 close-range rows sat off-centre and produced **essentially zero** identifications
despite being big enough in frame. Every one of the 1,170 identified rows across the whole
dataset falls in a narrow central band, cx 0.38–0.55 (median 0.459, 5th–95th percentile
0.42–0.51).

The U-shape is plainly visible in the position histogram, and it inverts the usual
assumption:

| Session | zone | share of rows | mean bbox fill |
|---|---|---|---|
| morning | centre | 52.6% | 0.28 |
| morning | far-right | 13.3% | **0.43** |
| afternoon | centre | 29.9% | 0.18 |
| afternoon | far-right | 28.3% | **0.28** |
| afternoon | left | 22.7% | 0.22 |

**Side-seated people are physically closer to the camera than centre-seated people, and
still were never identified.** For the arms of the U the limiting factor is not range at
all — it is **yaw angle**. They are seen in three-quarter or full profile, and the gallery
was built entirely from the 22-Jul bench test, which was 99.6% dead-centre, front-facing.
A frontal-only gallery cannot match a profile probe.

**Actions:**
- **Enrol each registered audience member from left ¾, front, and right ¾** (roughly
  −45°, 0°, +45°), and store all three embeddings under one identity. Match against the
  best of the set. This is cheap, needs no model change, and directly targets the ~47%
  (morning) to ~70% (afternoon) of detections that sit off-centre.
- Prefer a face model with **pose-robust embeddings** (ArcFace-family backbones trained
  with profile augmentation) over a frontal-tuned one.
- **Log estimated yaw per detection.** It cannot be recovered from the bbox, so this is
  currently unmeasurable — but the pose landmarks already computed (nose 0, eyes 1–6,
  ears 7–8) give a usable yaw estimate for free. Add it to `engagement_data.jsonl` so the
  next validation can confirm the angle hypothesis directly instead of inferring it from
  frame position.
- Combined with A3's track-level identity propagation, a side-seated person only needs to
  turn towards the camera once to have their whole track labelled.

**Caveat:** frame position is a proxy for viewing angle, not a measurement — a person on
the left of frame could still be facing the camera. The inference is strong here because
the effect survives controlling for apparent size (the table above holds size constant),
but the yaw logging above is what would settle it.

## A5. Is this the state of the art? — pixel budget first, model second

### A5.0 What we cannot see from here

- face **detector** (SCRFD? RetinaFace? YOLO-face? MediaPipe?) and its `det_size` /

- face **recogniser** and backbone (e.g. InsightFace `buffalo_l` = SCRFD + ArcFace
  `w600k_r50`, 512-d), and the training loss (ArcFace / AdaFace / CosFace / MagFace)
- the **match threshold** — observed similarities bottom out at 0.653, so it looks like
  ~0.65 cosine, which is loose for a gallery this small
- **camera model and native resolution**, and whether 640×480 was the sensor mode or a
  downscale in the pipeline
- whether inference ran on GPU (`device=auto` was logged, but not what it resolved to);
  observed 2.5–11.8 fps suggests CPU or a bottleneck

The pose stack *is* known: MediaPipe Holistic, 543 landmarks.

### A5.1 The binding constraint is resolution, and it is not close

Working backwards from the empirical threshold in this dataset (identification only fires
at bbox height ≥ 408 px), and taking head height ≈ 13% of standing body height:

| Session | median bbox height | **implied head height** | ID threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| morning | 126 px | **~16 px** | ~53 px |
| afternoon | 83 px | **~11 px** | ~53 px |

ArcFace-family recognisers take a 112×112 aligned crop. Published guidance for reliable
recognition is roughly **≥30 px inter-ocular distance** (ISO/IEC 19794-5 treats ~60 px
inter-eye as good quality, ~30 px as marginal); 16 px of *total head* corresponds to maybe
6–8 px between the eyes. **The information is not in the frame.** No model, no
super-resolution and no amount of tuning recovers identity from 6 px inter-ocular — SR
networks will simply invent a plausible face, which is worse than failing.

The empirical 53 px head threshold is a useful sanity check: it sits right where the
literature says recognition becomes marginal, which suggests the existing model is
performing about as expected and is **not** the thing that is broken.

Share of detection rows that would clear the ID threshold at higher sensor resolution,
same field of view:

| resolution | morning | afternoon |
|---|---|---|
| 640×480 (as captured) | 2.3% | 0.4% |
| 720p | 8.9% | 1.7% |
| 1080p | 28.7% | 11.3% |
| 1440p | 45.6% | 18.6% |
| **4K** | **79.1%** | **37.0%** |

**Conclusion: capture resolution is the number one lever, ahead of every model choice.**
1080p is the minimum worth attempting; 4K (or 1080p with roughly half the horizontal FOV)
is what actually solves it. Halving the FOV doubles pixels-on-face for free, so a longer
lens plus 1080p ≈ 4K at the current FOV — cheaper, at the cost of coverage. For a U-shaped
layout, two or three narrower-FOV cameras (one per arm, one centre) both fixes the pixel
budget and the yaw problem in A4 simultaneously, and is likely the best value option.

Other capture-side factors that cost effective resolution and are worth checking:
- **Compression**: heavy H.264 on a 640×480 stream destroys exactly the high-frequency
  facial detail the embedding depends on. Capture raw/MJPEG or a high bitrate.
- **Motion blur**: at 2.5–11.8 fps the exposure may be long. Faces need a short shutter;
  raise gain or add light rather than lengthening exposure.
- **Focus and lighting**: fixed focus set to the wrong plane, or a backlit stage washing
  out audience faces, both silently halve usable range.
- **Sensor mode**: confirm 640×480 is not a downscale of an available 1080p/4K stream —
  if it is, this is a one-line fix.

### A5.2 Model choices worth evaluating

Assuming the pixel budget gets fixed, the recogniser still matters. Verify current
benchmark standings before committing, but as of writing:

- **AdaFace** (CVPR 2022) is the most relevant family here: it adapts the margin by image
  quality (using feature norm as a quality proxy), and was explicitly designed for
  low-quality and low-resolution probes. It reports strong numbers on **TinyFace**, which
  is the benchmark that matches this use case.
- **ArcFace / CosFace / MagFace** with an **IResNet-100** backbone remain strong general
  baselines. InsightFace `buffalo_l` uses a smaller `r50`; moving to `r100` is a cheap
  accuracy gain if compute allows. **MagFace** additionally encodes quality in the
  embedding norm, which pairs well with the quality-weighted aggregation in A6.
- **Detector**: for small faces, detection usually fails before recognition does. SCRFD
  and YOLOv8-face variants handle small faces well, but only if `det_size` and the minimum
  face size are configured for it — defaults often discard faces under ~20 px. **Check
  how many faces are being detected at all** before blaming the recogniser.
- **Benchmarks to judge candidates on**: **TinyFace** and **QMUL-SurvFace** (native
  low-resolution surveillance), **SCFace** (multi-distance, closest analogue to this
  seating problem), and **IJB-B/IJB-C** (template-based, mixed quality). Ignore LFW —
  it is saturated and tells you nothing about range.
- Do **not** pick on headline LFW/MegaFace accuracy. Two models a fraction of a percent
  apart on frontal benchmarks can differ enormously at 30 px.

### A5.3 Realistic ceiling
Even fully optimised, expect identification to remain **opportunistic rather than
continuous** for a U-shaped audience at 5–10 m: people will be identified when they happen
to face the camera at sufficient size, and that identity then has to be *propagated* by
tracking (A3) rather than re-established every frame. Design the analysis around
per-track identity with confidence, not per-frame identity — and see **A7** for how to get
identity without relying on faces at all.

## A6. Accuracy techniques (independent of model choice)

These are the levers that usually beat swapping backbones, roughly in order of value:

1. **Exploit the closed set.** Only registered audience members matter (A2), so this is a
   *closed-set assignment* problem, not open-set verification. With N known people and M
   tracks, solve a global assignment (Hungarian) over the similarity matrix with a
   one-identity-per-track and one-track-per-identity constraint, instead of thresholding
   each face independently. This alone typically converts a large share of near-threshold
   matches into correct ones, because a weak match becomes decisive once every other
   candidate is ruled out.
2. **Quality-weighted template aggregation.** Build one template per track by averaging
   L2-normalised embeddings weighted by a quality score (feature norm works well with
   AdaFace/MagFace; otherwise use face size × detector confidence × frontality). Averaging
   over even 10–20 frames materially outperforms the single best frame.
3. **Calibrate the threshold on real data, not the default.** The apparent ~0.65 cutoff
   was never validated against this camera, lighting or gallery. Collect a labelled set,
   plot the ROC, and pick the operating point for the false-accept rate the study can
   tolerate. For research use, a *conservative* threshold plus "unknown" is far safer than
   a permissive one — a wrong identity silently corrupts the physiological pairing.
4. **Score normalisation** (z-norm / s-norm against a cohort) makes similarities
   comparable across people and lighting, which matters when one gallery image is better
   than the rest.
5. **Test-time augmentation**: average the embedding of the crop and its horizontal flip.
   Near-free, small but consistent gain — and it partially compensates for the left/right
   asymmetry in A4.
6. **Alignment quality.** ArcFace-family models are extremely sensitive to the 5-point
   similarity transform. Bad landmarks at small scale mean a misaligned crop and a useless
   embedding. Landmark confidence should gate whether a frame contributes at all.
7. **Frame gating.** Do not feed every frame to the recogniser. Score each candidate on
   size, sharpness (variance of Laplacian), frontality and landmark confidence, and only
   embed the top frames per track. Better accuracy *and* much cheaper — which also buys
   back the fps needed to run a heavier backbone.
8. **Multi-frame consistency.** Require k consecutive or k-of-n agreeing matches before
   committing an identity to a track, then lock it and stop re-querying.

## A7. Identity without faces — two strong options for this setup

Face recognition may be the wrong tool for a seated audience at 5–10 m. Two alternatives
fit this study unusually well, and both use data already being captured:

1. **Seat mapping.** A U-shaped layout means fixed positions. Register who sits where at
   the door, then assign identity from the track's *spatial position* and use the face
   only as confirmation when available. For a seated audience this is more reliable than
   face-ID at any resolution, and it degrades gracefully. It needs only a homography from
   the camera to a floor plan, which is a one-off calibration.
2. **Wearable-to-track motion matching.** The identities in this dataset *are* wearable
   IDs (`MD-V7-0000911`, `MD-V7-0001063`), so the real task is linking a track to a
   wearable — not recognising a face. Cross-correlate the EmotiBit accelerometer against
   the motion of the tracked body: the matching pair spikes, everything else does not.
   This works at *any* distance and *any* angle, needs no face at all, and both signals
   already exist — `raw_motion_*.csv` is populated for all 40 runs, and MediaPipe Holistic
   gives full-body landmarks.
   - **This is especially strong for the dyad design (see A8):** the infant wears the
     EmotiBit and is held by, or in contact with, the parent who is being tracked. When
     the infant is carried, the infant's accelerometer is measuring the *parent's* gross
     body motion — rocking, bouncing, swaying, walking. That is a large, distinctive,
     low-frequency signal, far easier to correlate than fine hand movement, and it is
     present exactly when face-ID is least likely to work.
   - Correlate infant accelerometer magnitude against the parent's torso/hip keypoint
     velocity (pose 11/12 shoulders, 23/24 hips), band-limited to ~0.5–3 Hz where rocking
     and bouncing live. Expect a clear winner against the handful of other candidate
     tracks.
   - Prerequisites: keypoints must actually be saved (**A1**) and the two capture clocks
     must agree (**issue 8** — currently 1 h apart), since cross-correlation needs a
     common time base. Both are already on this roadmap.
   - This is the most promising path for reliably pairing physiology to a person, and it
     sidesteps A3, A4 and A5 entirely.

## A8. Study design: infant wears the EmotiBit, parent is tracked on video

**Clarified 2026-08-05.** This reframes the identity problem and makes most of it easier:

- The **infant** wears the EmotiBit (physiology).
- The **parent** is tracked by the camera (pose, engagement).
- The infant is usually held by, or in direct contact with, the parent.

### What this changes

1. **The unit of analysis is the dyad, not the person.** Physiology and behaviour come
   from two different bodies by design. Analysis and file naming should carry a
   `dyad_id`, with `infant_device_id` and `parent_track_id` as attributes — never imply
   that one person produced both streams.
2. **Face-ID is only needed to tell parents apart**, and only as many parents as there are
   registered dyads — a very small closed set. Everything in A6.1 (Hungarian assignment
   over the gallery) becomes more powerful the smaller that set is. With, say, 6 dyads, a
   weak face match plus the exclusion of five alternatives is often decisive.
3. **Adults are the tracking target, so the pixel budget in A5.1 is the adult's.** Head
   height ≈13% of standing height still applies, so the resolution table stands.
4. **A7.2 becomes the primary identification route, not a fallback.** Physical contact
   between the wearable and the tracked body is exactly the condition under which
   accelerometer-to-pose correlation works best. This is the single most reliable way to
   bind a dyad, and it is immune to distance, angle and lighting.
5. **Proximity is a usable prior.** If an infant is detected at all, the nearest adult
   track is overwhelmingly likely to be its parent. Combined with the seat map (A7.1),
   dyads can be resolved with little or no face recognition.

### New risk this introduces: infants are detected but not labelled

The pose detector appears to be detecting held infants as additional people, inside the
parent's bounding box:

| Session | multi-person frames | frames with a small box >80% inside a larger one | distinct nested track pairs |
|---|---|---|---|
| morning | 48,256 | **8,294 (17.2%)** | 195 |
| afternoon | 19,806 | **5,849 (29.5%)** | 138 |

`people_count` reached 8 (morning) and 10 (afternoon) with medians of 5 and 7 — plausible
only if infants are being counted alongside adults.

**The problem is not that infants are detected — it is that nothing records what they
are.** Infant pose belongs in the audience gaze aggregate (see **A9**); it does not belong
in a headcount of registered adults. Without a role label, every aggregate is forced to
use the same population, and at least one of them will be wrong.

**Actions:**
- Add a **`role` field per track**: `adult`, `infant`, `performer`, `unknown`. Never drop
  a detection — label it, and let each metric choose its own population:

  | metric | adults | infants | performers |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | audience gaze / common focal point (**A9**) | yes | **yes** | **no** |
  | `people_count` (audience size) | yes | separate count | no |
  | `crowd_average` engagement | yes | yes | no |
  | face-ID gallery matching | yes | no | no |
  | dyad linking (**A7.2**) | yes | yes | no |

- Derive `infant` from containment plus size plus persistence over time (not a single
  frame), and from the dyad link below.
- Consider an explicit **dyad link** in the output: `parent_track_id` ↔ `infant_track_id`,
  maintained by containment and proximity over time. That link plus A7.2 gives a
  belt-and-braces binding of wearable to parent track — and it tells you when the infant
  is being held versus set down, which is itself a useful behavioural variable.
- **Caveat:** the containment test above is geometric, not a classifier — a distant adult
  standing directly behind a nearer one can trigger it. Verify against the video before
  trusting the percentages, and prefer a size-plus-containment-plus-persistence rule over
  containment alone.

## A9. Common gaze as the primary engagement measure

**Design decision (2026-08-05):** shared audience gaze is treated as the strongest
engagement indicator, with other behavioural cues secondary. **Infant pose is included in
the audience gaze aggregate.** Only performers are excluded.

### A9.1 Why this is the right call for this setup
- Joint/shared attention is a well-established attention marker in the developmental
  literature, so a common-gaze index is defensible as a construct, not just convenient.
- It is a **group** statistic. Individual head-direction estimates can be noisy and the
  aggregate still be stable, because error averages down across people while genuine
  shared attention does not. That is a real advantage at this resolution.
- Crucially, head orientation is a **geometric, body-scale cue** — it depends on where
  landmarks sit relative to each other, not on fine pixel texture. Unlike face
  identification (**A5.1**), it does not collapse at small face sizes. This is the one
  measure that can work at the distances involved.

### A9.2 What is measurable today, and what has to be switched on

Confirmed from the stored archives: the third keypoint channel is **visibility** for pose
landmarks (range 0–1) and **zero** for face landmarks (depth is not stored).

- **Available now — coarse head yaw from pose landmarks.** Nose (0), eyes (1–6) and ears
  (7, 8) with their visibility scores give yaw sign and rough magnitude: ear visibility
  asymmetry indicates which way the head is turned, and nose offset from the ear midpoint
  refines it. Cheap, and it degrades gracefully at small scales.
- **Available offline — full head pose (yaw/pitch/roll)** via `solvePnP` on the 468 stored
  2D face-mesh landmarks against the canonical MediaPipe face model. `solvePnP` needs only
  2D image points plus a canonical 3D model, so the missing depth channel is not a
  blocker. It does require the face mesh to have been detected at all, which at 11–16 px
  head height will often fail — log which method produced each estimate.
- **Not available — eye/iris gaze.** 543 = 33 pose + 468 face + 21 + 21 hands. Iris
  refinement produces 478 face landmarks (553 total), so `refine_landmarks=True` was off.
  Turn it on to capture the 10 iris points — but treat iris gaze as a **stretch goal
  contingent on A5.1**, because it needs far more resolution than head pose does. Head
  direction is the realistic signal here.
- All of this is computed offline from saved keypoints, so it depends on **A1**.

### A9.3 The metric — and a trap specific to the U-shape

Represent each audience member's gaze as a direction and use circular statistics:

- **Mean resultant length** $R = \left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{i\theta_j}\right| \in [0, 1]$
  is the common-gaze index: $R \to 1$ means everyone is looking the same way, $R \to 0$
  means dispersed. Report $R$ over a sliding window (~2–5 s), alongside the mean direction
  and circular variance $1 - R$.
- Weight each contribution by confidence (landmark visibility × head size) so distant or
  uncertain heads count for less, and record how many people contributed to each window —
  suppress the value when $N$ is too small.

**The trap:** in a U-shaped layout, two people on opposite arms looking at the *same*
performer have very different angles *in the camera frame*. Computed naively, genuine
shared attention will look like total disagreement. **Camera-frame angles are not
comparable across seats in a U.**

The fix is to work in room coordinates: project each gaze ray onto the stage/floor plane
via a homography and measure the **dispersion of the intersection points**. That yields an
actual focal *point* with a spread, which is what "mean audience gaze focal point"
should mean. It needs a one-off calibration — mark four known points on the floor, or
shoot a checkerboard — and it is a prerequisite for the metric being meaningful rather
than an artefact of seating position. It also pays for itself immediately in A9.4 and
A7.1.

### A9.4 Excluding performers — ranked discriminators

Your four cues map onto these, easiest and most robust first:

1. **Register them manually, and use a spatial zone.** You know who the performers are
   before the session starts. A manual role assignment plus a polygon exclusion zone over
   the open end of the U (using the A9.3 homography) is nearly free and close to perfect.
   **Do this first** — everything below is inference to cover what it misses.
2. **Standing vs seated.** Hip/knee/ankle geometry and visibility, plus head height above
   the floor plane once the homography exists. Note that in this dataset the seated
   subject's hips and ankles had visibility ≈0.00 while shoulders were ≈0.9 — that
   contrast is itself a usable seated/standing signal.
3. **Motion energy.** Windowed variance of keypoint velocity. Performers move more; cheap
   and robust, and it needs no calibration.
4. **Facing direction.** Performers face into the U, roughly opposite the audience mean.
   **Careful — this is circular:** do not use a person's gaze to decide whether they
   belong in the gaze aggregate that defines the reference direction. Seed the audience
   mean from the exclusion zone, then refine iteratively, and never let this cue act
   alone.
5. **Instrument held/played.** Hardest and least necessary. Options are a fine-tuned
   object detector, zero-shot detection (OWL-ViT / CLIP) on the person crop, or a pose
   signature such as sustained asymmetric arm elevation with hands apart at chest height.
   Lowest priority — 1–3 will already have solved it.

Combine into a per-track role score with hysteresis so roles cannot flip frame to frame,
and persist the decision on the track (**A3** makes tracks stable enough for this to hold).

### A9.5 Retraining the engagement model

**Unknown from here:** the current model's architecture, features and training labels are
not recorded anywhere in the logs. Report those before planning a retrain — the right
move may be to reweight rather than rebuild.

**Define the construct before touching the model.** Proposed channels:
- *Primary:* individual head direction toward the performer, and the group common-gaze
  index $R$ from A9.3.
- *Secondary:* stillness / quiet attention; rhythmic movement entrained to the beat;
  postural orientation toward the stage; hand and arm movement to music.
- *Dyad-specific:* infant gaze **to the parent** rather than the performer. For an infant
  this is meaningful behaviour — dyadic versus joint attention — **not noise**, and it
  should be a labelled state, not discarded.

**Labels are the gate on all of this.** Without them "retrained" is unfalsifiable.
- Human-annotate video against a defined coding scheme, and report inter-rater
  reliability (Cohen's κ or ICC) on a double-coded subset.
- Adopt an existing infant engagement/attention coding scheme from the developmental
  literature rather than inventing one — it makes the work publishable and comparable.

**Weak supervision you already have, for free:** the audio state log gives
`music_detected`, `singing_detected` and `state` at 0.5 s resolution. Engagement should
co-vary with musical structure, so MUSIC/SINGING onsets are weak positives and PAUSE
stretches are weak negatives. That is enough to pretrain or regularise a model before any
manual annotation exists.

**Do not train on the infant physiology.** If HR/IBI is the study's outcome variable, then
using it as a training target for the engagement model makes any later
engagement-predicts-physiology finding circular. Keep it strictly on the outcome side.

**Architecture and validation:**
- A small temporal model (temporal CNN / GRU) over a 2–10 s window of per-person features,
  plus a set-level aggregation for the group measure. Keep the per-person model modest —
  the group statistic does the heavy lifting.
- **Hold out whole sessions, never frames.** At ~10 fps, adjacent frames are near-duplicates
  and a frame-level split will leak badly and report inflated accuracy.
- Keep the current score as a baseline to compare against, and stamp a model version into
  the output so earlier sessions stay interpretable.
- Whatever replaces the warm-up buffer must **degrade gracefully**. A measure that emits
  nothing at all (P0-1) is worse than a noisy one carrying a confidence band.

### A9.6 Honest constraints
- At 11–16 px head height, even coarse yaw will be noisy. The group statistic mitigates
  this but does not remove it — **resolution (A5.1) is the top lever for gaze too**, not
  just for identity.
- Seated U-shapes occlude: people in front block those behind. Mounting the camera higher
  gives a cleaner line to every head and directly improves both gaze and tracking.
- Report coverage alongside every gaze value — what fraction of present audience members
  actually yielded an estimate. A high $R$ computed from three of nine people is not a
  finding.

# PART B — Analysis findings (this dataset)

## P0 — Blocks analysis of this dataset

### 1. Engagement scores never converged
- Morning session `2026-07-26_08-55-18`: `buffer_fill` peaked at **0.42**; only **0.33%**
  of 208,549 rows carry a non-zero score.
- Afternoon session `2026-07-26_14-27-40`: `buffer_fill` **0.00 throughout**; **zero** of
  140,519 rows scored.
- Cause: tracker churn. 373 and 221 distinct `track_id`s for at most 8–10 people; median
  track lifetime ~20 s. The temporal buffer resets on every re-ID, so it never fills.
- **Action:** persist the score buffer across track-ID changes (key on re-identified
  person, not raw track ID), and/or raise the tracker `max_age` / lower the re-ID
  threshold. A Kalman-predicted bbox with proper association (SORT / ByteTrack / OC-SORT)
  is the standard fix — see **A3**, which depends on this same change. Re-run once fixed;
  the raw detections are intact, so scores can be recomputed offline from the JSONL if the
  scorer is refactored to accept replay input.

### 2. Face-ID range is ~2.2 m — pre-ship concern confirmed

  were ever identified, and **all** of them are from the 22-Jul close-up bench test.
